# Investment Standard

Status: Canonical v1 governance  
Primary user: Stage B investment analysis. Also used by memo verification and media scoring.

## Purpose

New Management is pre-NDA underwriting, not faux diligence.

The job is to make the best possible investment judgment from what a buyer can know publicly before disclosure, then identify the few things that would most change that judgment.

The core question is:

**Can this business be taken to the next level as an attractive target? If so, how?**

## The three analytical buckets

### 1. Knowable pre-NDA

Research and calculate these properly when material.

Examples:

- asking price;
- stated revenue;
- stated SDE or EBITDA;
- owner involvement if stated;
- employees;
- assets;
- real estate or rent;
- recurring mix if stated;
- years established;
- reason for sale if stated;
- seller financing;
- broad category economics;
- likely capital intensity;
- demand backdrop;
- owner replacement economics;
- broad valuation grounding;
- illustrative debt service and equity economics.

### 2. Inferable, but illustrative

Use explicit ranges and category assumptions.

Examples:

- gross margin when not disclosed;
- maintenance capex;
- working-capital character;
- owner replacement cost;
- incremental margins;
- pricing uplift;
- territory expansion;
- category revenue mix;
- replacement management cost.

Write these as our assumptions, not company facts.

### 3. Diligence-dependent

Do not research these into fake certainty.

Examples:

- customer concentration;
- actual churn;
- detailed addbacks;
- exact working capital;
- receivables quality;
- contract terms;
- deferred maintenance;
- employee retention;
- backlog quality;
- lease clauses;
- owner-held relationships.

Convert material unknowns into What Has to Be True and Swing Questions.

## Four layers of judgment

### 1. Business quality

Is this a good economic machine?

Use the Quality Card and Business Physics.

### 2. Deal quality

Is it attractive at the asking price or at a plausible price?

Use normalized economics, valuation grounding, effective multiple, financing, and downside.

### 3. Ownership structure

What does it take to own this business successfully?

What functions must transfer? What must the next owner be unusually good at? Can a management layer support the desired ownership model?

### 4. Personal fit

Only where material.

This is not the public center of the analysis. Full relocation, tax, or personal take-home modeling is outside v1 unless a specific deal warrants it.

## Business quality

Assess the attributes that matter to this business, not every possible attribute.

Core lenses:

- gross margin;
- normalized profitability;
- revenue durability;
- operating leverage;
- working-capital intensity;
- capital intensity;
- demand backdrop;
- natural expansion;
- pricing power;
- transferability.

The Quality Card provides the reading. The prose must explain the mechanism.

No overall weighted score.

## Business Physics

Reduce the business to the few variables that drive the economics.

Name:

- the operating equation or mental model;
- what binds growth;
- what is scarce.

Examples of driving variables may include:

- crews × productive hours × realized revenue per hour × utilization;
- technicians × stops per day × average revenue per stop;
- machines × available hours × utilization × contribution per machine-hour;
- seats × turns × average check × open days.

The formula does not need to be mathematically perfect. It needs to expose the economic machine.

## What binds

Identify the constraint that most limits growth or economics today.

Examples:

- technician availability;
- route density;
- machine capacity;
- sales capacity;
- working capital;
- licenses;
- real estate;
- owner judgment;
- customer acquisition;
- throughput.

Do not assume the most visible problem is the binding constraint.

## What is scarce

Identify the resource that creates economic value because it is difficult to replicate.

Examples:

- dense customer routes;
- licensed labor;
- a permit;
- long-held customer relationships;
- local reputation;
- specialized equipment;
- distribution access;
- a referral channel;
- operating know-how.

Scarcity should explain economics, not merely sound strategic.

## Normalization

Stated SDE or EBITDA is a starting point.

Reconstruct what the business plausibly earns after replacing the owner's actual work and accounting for economic costs that matter.

Potential adjustments include:

- questionable addbacks;
- owner replacement cost;
- maintenance capex;
- non-operating income;
- real estate income if excluded from the operating business;
- unusual or clearly nonrecurring items where publicly supportable.

Never apply a mechanical GM salary simply because the owner exists.

Price the functions the owner actually performs.

## Reconstructed P&L

Use three columns:

1. **As Listed**
2. **Year 0, Our View**
3. **New Management Case, Year 3**

Rows may be:

- point estimate;
- range;
- not enough evidence.

The exhibit carries evidence coverage and reconstruction confidence.

The third column may move only through mechanisms actually identified in Business Physics and Under New Management.

## Valuation hierarchy

Use valuation in this order.

### 1. Headline multiple

Ask divided by stated SDE or EBITDA.

This is what the listing appears to cost.

### 2. Market grounding

Use broad category and size-band market indicators from credible published sources.

Treat them as relative grounding, not perfect comps.

Do not hunt for false precision in individual SMB precedent transactions.

### 3. Our normalization

Estimate what earnings plausibly survive on an owner-independent basis.

### 4. Implied effective multiple

Recalculate the asking price against normalized Year 0 cash earnings.

A deal advertised at 3.1x SDE can be economically expensive if only part of that SDE survives normalization.

This number often matters more than the broker multiple.

## Year 0 value versus New Management value

The Year 0 column is what the buyer pays for.

The New Management Case is the return.

Never add our value-creation ideas into the seller's value.

Pre-NDA, state:

- an indicative Year 0 value range;
- a preliminary maximum price.

A true walk-away price belongs after disclosure.

## Financing

Keep financing arithmetic simple and useful.

Illustrate:

- purchase price;
- debt;
- seller note where relevant;
- equity;
- Year 0 normalized cash earnings;
- annual debt service;
- debt-service coverage;
- cash to equity.

The financing base case uses the asking price and the Year 0 cash-earnings point estimate. If Year 0 is a range with no defensible point estimate, use the midpoint and say that explicitly.

Show a deterministic sensitivity grid across three price cases:

- **Asking Price** = the current asking price;
- **Year 0 Value Low** = the low end of our indicative Year 0 value range;
- **Preliminary Maximum** = our preliminary maximum price.

Use three earnings cases:

- **Downside** = approximately 20% below the base Year 0 earnings case;
- **Base** = the Year 0 point estimate or range midpoint used in the financing base case;
- **Upside** = approximately 20% above the base Year 0 earnings case.

If two price cases are numerically identical, keep the labels rather than inventing a different price merely to create dispersion.

Exit analysis is optional. Use it only when it adds information.

Do not make a universal Year 5 model the center of a small-business memo.

## Transaction setup

Ask why this business may be available at this price now, but stay within the evidence.

Use stated facts such as:

- asking price;
- seller financing;
- reason for sale;
- broker language;
- unusual terms;
- obvious structural features.

Do not invent seller urgency or motive.

## The Bet

State the provisional underwriting hypothesis in one or two sentences.

It should identify:

- what may be unusually attractive or misread;
- what must be true for that interpretation to hold.

Research may rewrite or kill the Bet.

The Price section closes by answering:

**Did the Bet survive?**

## Under New Management

Use governance/08_value_creation_standard.md.

The value-creation case should be tied to Business Physics, quantified where useful, and separated from Year 0 value.

## Bear Case

Build the strongest plausible case against the investment.

Ask:

- how the Bet could be wrong;
- where earnings may be overstated;
- what could fail to transfer;
- which fragilities are concentrated;
- what is left if the thesis fails.

Downside protection may come from assets, contracts, licenses, real estate, relationships, or strategic value.

Sometimes there is little protection. Say so.

## What Has to Be True

The analysis should identify the three to seven company-specific questions whose answers could materially:

- kill the deal;
- reprice the deal;
- materially strengthen the thesis;
- refine the thesis.

Do not substitute a generic diligence checklist.

A Swing Question is valuable because it tests this Bet.

## Ownership

State:

- what the next owner must be unusually good at;
- what functions are transferable;
- whether a management layer exists or must be created;
- what ownership structures appear feasible.

Do not equate "semi-absentee" listing language with owner independence.

## Archetypes and overlays

Archetypes and overlays are optional reading aids.

Examples:

- Cash Cow;
- Fixer;
- Mispriced Asset;
- Platform;
- Thematic Tailwind;
- Tech Lift;
- Remote-Capable.

Do not force a business into an archetype if it adds little.

The analysis matters more than the label.

## Things v1 deliberately does not do

Do not spend large amounts of effort on:

- reconstructing an undisclosed balance sheet;
- precise working capital from nothing;
- pretending to know churn;
- precise gross-margin decimals unsupported by evidence;
- exhaustive company-level transaction comps;
- rigid five-year forecasts;
- filling every P&L cell;
- diligence questions that cannot be answered publicly.

The objective is not completeness.

The objective is a better judgment.

## Final investment test

A strong Stage B output should allow a reader to answer:

- What is this business really about?
- Why does it make money?
- What constrains it?
- What is unusually good or bad?
- What does the advertised earnings number actually mean?
- What am I really paying?
- What could I change?
- Why might those changes work?
- How could I be wrong?
- What few things do I need to learn next?
