# Value Creation Standard

Status: Canonical v1 governance  
Primary users: investment analysis, memo writer, memo verifier.

## Purpose

Under New Management is where the analysis stops describing the business and starts earning the name of the publication.

The standard is not "find ways to increase revenue and decrease costs."

The standard is to identify specific changes that follow from the economics of this particular business, state what must be true for them to work, explain why the current owner has not already captured them, and show how they change the business.

## The quality ladder

Value-creation ideas generally fall on three rungs.

### Generic

Names a direction but not a mechanism.

Examples:

- raise prices;
- improve marketing;
- add technology;
- hire salespeople;
- expand geographically.

Generic ideas do not pass.

### Mechanical

Identifies the operational mechanism, target, and economic effect.

Example:

> Reprice the maintenance customers whose rates have not reset in several years, using contract-renewal dates rather than an across-the-board increase, and test churn by cohort before extending the change.

This is actionable and tied to the P&L.

Every surfaced lever must reach at least this rung.

### Structural

Changes the architecture or quality of the business, not merely a line item.

Example:

> Move the maintenance book from informal repeat service to a standardized auto-renewing service agreement with indexed pricing. If customers accept it, the value is larger than the price increase itself: recurrence becomes more durable, revenue visibility improves, and the business may deserve a better multiple.

Structural ideas are valuable when they are real. They are not required.

## Follow the Business Physics

A good lever should move a variable that actually drives the business.

Examples:

- price per job;
- jobs per route;
- technician utilization;
- throughput per machine;
- revenue per square foot;
- customer retention;
- acquisition cost;
- attachment rate;
- inventory turns;
- collection days;
- manager span;
- territory density.

If an idea does not connect to the economic machine, ask whether it belongs.

## Silent search space

The analysis should consider relevant lever families internally:

- pricing architecture;
- customer-acquisition channel;
- service adjacency;
- route or territory density;
- labor model;
- technology;
- working-capital terms;
- brand and positioning;
- ownership and management structure;
- M&A.

This is a search space, not a checklist to display.

Surface only the levers that genuinely matter to the specific business.

## The two gates

Every material lever must answer two questions.

### 1. What has to be true?

State the conditions under which the lever works.

A lever with no falsifiable conditions is usually too vague.

### 2. Why has the current owner not already done it?

Possible explanations include:

- capital constraints;
- age or retirement timing;
- lifestyle preference;
- limited management capacity;
- lack of expertise;
- incentive misalignment;
- execution burden;
- historical neglect;
- the opportunity may not actually exist.

Do not invent the owner's motive.

If the reason is not publicly known, frame plausible explanations as hypotheses and make the answer part of what must be verified.

A weak answer to either gate lowers confidence or removes the lever.

## The bank test

Bold ideas are welcome. Gimmicks are not.

Ask:

**Would a serious operator take this idea into an operating plan or financing discussion?**

If the answer is no, the idea is probably content theater rather than investment thinking.

## Originality without forced novelty

Actively search for at least one differentiated lever a typical buyer may miss.

Include it only if it survives the same evidentiary and economic tests as every other lever.

If none survives, say so rather than manufacture novelty.

The verifier explicitly checks any purported differentiated lever for this.

The goal is not to be surprising. The goal is to notice something real that others may not.

## Day 1, Year 1, Year 3

Under New Management should tell a time-based operating story.

### Day 1: what we leave alone

Often the most important decision.

Identify what is already working and should not be disrupted.

A buyer who changes everything is not automatically more ambitious.

### Year 1: what we change

Concrete operating moves with:

- mechanism;
- cost;
- timeline;
- confidence;
- first 90-day action;
- what has to be true;
- why it may be uncaptured today.

### Year 3: what business we are trying to create

Describe the economic destination.

This is not a five-year strategic-plan exercise.

The point is to show how the business could become different in quality, scale, durability, or cash generation if the chosen levers work.

## Earnings bridge

Every quantified lever should appear in the New Management earnings bridge.

For example:

Year 0 cash earnings: $420K  
Pricing architecture: +$70K  
Route density: +$90K  
Adjacent service: +$120K  
Added management: -$60K  
New Management Case: approximately $640K

The bridge is illustrative, not a promise.

Ranges are preferred when the evidence does not support a point estimate.

Do not hide the cost required to create the upside.

## Why the owner has not captured it matters

A value-creation idea is weaker if there is no credible reason it remains available.

Sometimes the answer is that a buyer has no special edge and the idea is probably obvious.

That is useful information.

The section should distinguish:

- **available because the current owner rationally chose not to pursue it;**
- **available because the next owner may bring a different capability;**
- **available only if an unverified condition proves true;**
- **probably not available at all.**

## Do not pay for our own ideas

Year 0 value comes from the business as it exists.

Under New Management is the return.

If a seller's asking price already assumes the upside we must execute to create, say so.

## Output standard for each lever

A strong surfaced lever should make clear:

- what changes;
- which Business Physics variable it moves;
- why the mechanism should work;
- rough economic impact or range;
- cost required;
- time to impact;
- confidence;
- first 90-day action;
- what has to be true;
- why it has not already happened.

This information can be written naturally. It does not need to appear as a rigid ten-field checklist in the prose.

## Final test

A strong Under New Management section should make a serious operator think:

**That is specific enough to try, grounded enough to underwrite, and interesting enough that I would not have seen all of it from the listing alone.**
