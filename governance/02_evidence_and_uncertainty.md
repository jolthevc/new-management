# Evidence and Uncertainty Standard

Status: Canonical v1 governance  
Primary users: research, investment analysis, memo writing, memo verification, article claim checking.

## Purpose

New Management makes strong judgments from incomplete pre-NDA information. This file defines how to do that without turning inference into fact or uncertainty into fake precision.

A missing fact is not permission to guess. It is also not automatically a negative.

## The four evidence classes

Every material company-specific claim belongs to one of four classes.

### 1. Observed

A fact explicitly stated in the public listing, source email, or an independent public source.

Examples:

- The listing asks $2.4 million.
- The listing states $720,000 of SDE.
- The company website lists three service locations.
- A state licensing database shows an active license.

Observed facts should be written as facts and retain a traceable source.

### 2. Inferred

Our analytical conclusion drawn from observed evidence.

An inference must include the reason.

Good:

> We suspect route density is important here because the company serves a compact metro area with a field-service model, but the listing does not disclose customer locations.

Bad:

> The company has excellent route density.

The second sentence converts an analytical possibility into an invented company fact.

If an inference materially affects the thesis, valuation, or New Management Case, it should usually become a Swing Question.

### 3. Category Assumption

A reasonable assumption about the business type or operating model that is not established for this specific company.

Examples:

- a maintenance-capex range for a service fleet;
- a typical gross-margin range for a category;
- a plausible replacement salary for a role;
- common working-capital behavior in a type of project business.

Category assumptions must be labeled as such. They should come from Stage A research or an approved reusable category reference if one exists.

A category assumption can support an illustrative range. It cannot silently become a company-specific fact.

### 4. Must Verify

A material fact that cannot responsibly be established from the available public evidence.

Common examples:

- customer concentration;
- actual retention or churn;
- exact contract terms;
- detailed addbacks;
- true working-capital needs;
- receivables quality;
- deferred maintenance;
- employee retention;
- owner-held relationships;
- backlog quality;
- lease clauses not publicly disclosed.

Must Verify is a valid analytical result.

The right response is usually to convert the unknown into a specific question that tests the thesis.

## Missing disclosure

Missing disclosure is an open question, not evidence of weakness.

Do not write:

> Customer concentration is a risk.

when the actual evidence is:

> The listing does not disclose customer concentration.

Instead explain why the missing fact matters to this particular bet and what answer would strengthen or weaken the thesis.

## Research discipline

Research exists to improve the investment judgment, not to create the appearance of completeness.

Stage A should begin with four to seven questions that are material to the specific deal.

Research only what can change our understanding of:

- the economic machine;
- normalization;
- valuation;
- the New Management Case;
- the Bear Case;
- the Swing Questions.

Do not research every possible dimension simply because it is available.

When a question cannot be answered credibly from public sources, say so and stop.

## Source quality

Prefer the most direct and authoritative source that is practical for the question.

Examples include:

- government and regulatory sources;
- company or marketplace materials for facts they directly state;
- industry associations;
- primary market data;
- reputable research and trade publications;
- credible local sources for local facts.

A weak source does not become strong because several websites repeat it.

When evidence is mixed, preserve the disagreement rather than smoothing it into one number.

## Numbers

Every material number should be one of:

- observed;
- deterministically calculated from observed numbers;
- an explicitly labeled range;
- a category assumption;
- not enough evidence.

Do not invent a point estimate because a downstream table expects one.

The Reconstructed P&L explicitly supports three row states:

- point estimate;
- range;
- not enough evidence.

Use them.

## Traceability

The chain should be mechanically traceable:

**research question → sourced finding → evidence class → analytical claim → memo claim → article claim**

Structured schemas exist to preserve that chain.

A verifier should be able to answer:

- Where did this claim come from?
- At what certainty was it originally stated?
- Did the wording become stronger downstream?
- Did a number change?
- Did research that never survived into the approved memo leak into the article?

If the answer cannot be traced, the claim does not pass.

## Publication eligibility

Publication eligibility follows provenance.

Eligible:

- public listing facts;
- public source-email facts;
- independent public research;
- clearly labeled category assumptions;
- analytical inferences that preserve their uncertainty.

Ineligible:

- confidential disclosure;
- NDA material;
- private seller or broker communication not intended for publication;
- any fact whose provenance is unclear.

The article may use only substance that survived into the approved memo.

The claim checker may read the full research pack for verification, but a fact that appears in research and not in the approved memo is **new substance** and must be flagged.

## Writing uncertainty well

Uncertainty should be precise, not timid.

Avoid hedge stacks such as:

> It may perhaps potentially be the case that...

Prefer:

> We do not know the customer mix. If the top three accounts drive half the revenue, the thesis changes.

The goal is not to sound cautious. The goal is to be exact about the boundary of knowledge.

## Strong inference is allowed

This standard is not a ban on reasoning.

New Management should make strong analytical inferences when the evidence supports them.

The rule is simple:

**The strength of the language cannot exceed the strength of the evidence.**

## Named-listing fairness

The article concerns an identifiable real listing.

Before publication, ask:

- Would this sentence be fair if the owner read it?
- Is a negative statement framed as a fact we know or a question a buyer should verify?
- Are we criticizing economics rather than assigning motives?
- Are our estimates visibly ours?
- Have we reproduced expressive listing copy rather than restated facts in our own language?

Honest analysis can be critical. It should not be careless.
