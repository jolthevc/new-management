# Editorial Standard

Status: Canonical v1 governance  
Primary users: memo writer, media writer, memo verifier, media scorer.

## North star

New Management should make serious business analysis feel easy to read.

The prose should be knowledgeable without feeling institutional, rigorous without becoming homework, and playful enough that ordinary businesses feel as interesting as they actually are.

The governing aspiration:

**Dense ideas, light reading.**

The intellectual density can be high. The visual and narrative density should not be.

The memo and article share the same prose standard. The memo carries more scaffolding, exhibits, and evidence distinctions, but it does not get permission to sound like an internal banking document.

## How to think about style

Hard rules are for truth, sourcing, math, and uncertainty.

Style is governed primarily by principles and examples.

Do not optimize the prose for compliance with a long list of micro-rules. That produces safe, rigid writing.

Use the examples in /examples/prose_gold_standard.md as the primary tonal reference.

Consistency should come from taste and quality of thought, not identical sentence shapes.

## The basic movement

When a passage is doing real analytical work, a useful movement is:

**specific fact → underlying mechanism → investment consequence → transferable insight → implication for value or price**

This is a thinking pattern, not a paragraph template.

Use it when it helps. Do not force every paragraph through the same sequence.

## What good prose does

Good New Management prose:

- starts from something concrete;
- explains why it matters;
- follows the mechanism instead of announcing a conclusion;
- develops a view rather than naming one;
- uses plain language to carry finance;
- allows the reader to see how the conclusion was earned;
- teaches something transferable without switching into lesson mode;
- treats excellent businesses with as much analytical curiosity as flawed ones;
- knows when a sentence is enough and when an idea needs room;
- sounds authored rather than assembled.

The reader should forget that they are reading "analysis."

## Readability and pacing

Write for the eye as well as the mind.

Paragraph breaks should follow the movement of thought.

A paragraph should generally do one meaningful piece of work: establish an observation, explain a mechanism, develop an implication, challenge the thesis, or land a conclusion.

When the thought turns, the paragraph should usually turn with it.

Do not compress several distinct ideas into one wall of text merely because they are related.

Do not manufacture drama with constant one-line paragraphs either.

The page should naturally contain:

- short paragraphs that land a point;
- medium paragraphs that develop an idea;
- occasional longer paragraphs when the mechanism genuinely needs room.

Sections should vary in length and shape.

Use bullets when the information is genuinely list-shaped.

Use a table or exhibit when numbers are easier to understand visually.

A table should remove numerical burden from the prose, not force the prose to repeat every number in the table.

### The phone test

If a smart reader opens the piece on a phone and sees a large uninterrupted wall of text, look for the natural turn in the argument.

The solution is not an arbitrary sentence limit. It is better editorial judgment.

## Rhythm

Create verbal and visual rhythm.

A dense mechanism explanation may be followed by a short implication.

A quantitative exhibit may be followed by a narrative paragraph that tells the reader what matters.

A long section may be followed by a brief one.

Strong sentences may occasionally stand alone when they genuinely deserve the space.

Do not make every section structurally identical.

## Point of view

Every major section should have something to say.

The Quality Card can state the reading. The prose should explain why the reading is true, why it matters, and how it affects the deal.

Avoid sections that merely summarize data already visible in an exhibit.

## Positive depth

The system should not drift into "here is the catch."

If a business is excellent, explain the mechanism of excellence.

Do not write:

> Recurring revenue is attractive.

Explain what holds the revenue in place, what it does to operating leverage, what can break it, and what that durability deserves in the price.

Finding hidden value is as important as finding hidden risk.

## Tone

The tone is:

- curious;
- confident;
- approachable;
- specific;
- occasionally playful;
- respectful;
- commercially literate;
- comfortable making a judgment.

It is not:

- corporate;
- academic;
- breathless;
- snarky;
- performatively clever;
- faux-casual;
- padded with finance jargon.

Critique the business model and the deal, never the owner.

## Common machine-prose tells

These are warnings, not a banned-word dictionary.

Watch for:

- throat-clearing section openings;
- mechanical "first, second, third" construction when the ideas are not truly sequential;
- phrases such as "it is worth noting" or "from an investment perspective";
- rhetorical questions used as automatic transitions;
- closing sentences that simply restate the paragraph;
- stacked hedges;
- repeated "not X, but Y" constructions;
- identical sentence cadence across several paragraphs;
- symmetrical lists that sound generated rather than observed;
- adjectives standing in for explanation;
- generic endings such as "additional diligence would be required";
- walls of text;
- the opposite affectation of constant one-line paragraphs.

The verifier and media scorer should notice these patterns by name, but they should not flatten lively prose merely to avoid them.

## Hooks

An opening should make the reader curious about the particular business.

Good hooks often begin with:

- a surprising mechanism;
- a contradiction in the listing;
- an unexpectedly good economic feature;
- a physical detail that explains the business;
- a number whose meaning is different from what it first appears;
- a simple question the article will actually answer.

Avoid generic openings about how large an industry is or how "small businesses are the backbone of America."

## Length

Length is governed by what the idea earns.

For public articles, most pieces will naturally land around **1,800 to 2,600 words of body prose**, excluding source cards, exhibits, captions, image briefs, methodology, sources, and polls.

This is a center of gravity, not a minimum or maximum.

A straightforward business may work at 1,400 to 1,700 words.

A genuinely rich analysis may deserve 3,000 or more.

The rule:

**Never remove material insight to hit a target. Never expand thin analysis to reach one.**

The memo will often be longer because it retains analytical scaffolding, Swing Questions, exhibits, and evidence distinctions. Roughly 2,500 to 4,000 words may be common. There is no meaningful hard cap.

Length itself is not quality. Editorial judgment about length is.

## Visual publication character

New Management should not look like HBR research, an investment-banking deck, or a generic AI newsletter.

The visual north star:

**Approachable enough that you want to look at it. Smart enough that you never feel talked down to. Playful enough that business analysis stops feeling like homework.**

The analysis is adult. The curiosity can be almost childlike.

### House illustration style

The default editorial-image family is:

- warm, hand-drawn, authored illustration;
- gouache and colored-pencil character;
- tactile cream or paper-like ground;
- cheerful but controlled color;
- simplified people and objects;
- slightly whimsical proportions;
- occasional miniature-world compositions;
- clear visual ideas that read quickly;
- small moments of humor where appropriate;
- fascination with the physical world of ordinary businesses.

The publication should enjoy vans, ladders, machines, ovens, uniforms, pallets, storefronts, tools, routes, crews, inventory, waiting rooms, counters, kitchens, and factory floors.

Avoid:

- corporate vector art;
- sterile infographic aesthetics;
- glossy photorealistic AI imagery;
- overly serious monochrome editorial art;
- random style changes between articles;
- visual complexity whose main job is to signal intelligence.

### What generated images are for

A public article may contain roughly two to four AI-image opportunities when useful.

They generally serve one of three roles:

**SCENE**  
Establish the physical world of the business and give the reader a visual reset.

**MECHANISM**  
Make an operating or economic mechanism intuitive.

**TRANSFORMATION**  
Make an important Under New Management idea tangible.

These are roles, not required slots.

Do not add an image because a word-count threshold was reached.

### Defensibility

Generated imagery is editorial illustration, not documentary evidence.

Never imply that an illustration depicts:

- the seller's actual facility;
- actual employees;
- exact equipment;
- branded vehicles;
- a real storefront;
- a company logo;
- any other undisclosed company-specific fact.

### Standard inline image brief

The media writer inserts an image opportunity where it belongs in the article using this structure:

**[AI IMAGE — ROLE]**

**Idea:** What the reader should understand or feel, expressed as a visual concept.  
**Purpose:** Why the visual earns its place.  
**Style:** New Management house illustration style.  
**Avoid:** Any company-specific or documentary implication, plus any scene-specific exclusions.  
**Aspect:** Usually 3:2 landscape for inline art.

The brief should leave meaningful compositional freedom to the image-generation step.

Example:

**[AI IMAGE — MECHANISM]**

**Idea:** Route density is the hidden superpower of this business. Show two playful miniature neighborhoods from above: one service van zig-zagging absurdly across town, another making a neat cluster of stops on neighboring streets. The idea should read instantly and carry a little visual humor.  
**Purpose:** Make the operating-leverage mechanism intuitive before the reader sees the math.  
**Style:** New Management house illustration style, warm hand-drawn gouache and colored-pencil character on tactile cream paper, cheerful controlled palette, slightly whimsical proportions.  
**Avoid:** Photorealism, corporate vector style, company logos, readable fake text, or anything implying this depicts the actual seller.  
**Aspect:** 3:2 landscape.

## Analytical exhibits

The numbers remain rigorous even when the presentation is friendly.

Quality Card, Reconstructed P&L, and earnings bridge should use:

- clear hierarchy;
- generous space;
- legible labels;
- minimal decorative noise;
- a visual character consistent with the publication where it does not interfere with comprehension.

Do not make the analytical exhibits look like pasted-in investment-banking output.

Do not make them cute at the expense of clarity.

## Final test

A strong passage should feel like someone intelligent noticed something specific, followed it until it mattered, and then explained it without making the reader work harder than necessary.
