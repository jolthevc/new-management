# Prose Gold Standard

Status: Living reference  
Purpose: Show what excellent New Management writing feels like.

Use these examples more heavily than a long style checklist.

Do not imitate their sentence shapes mechanically. Imitate the quality of observation, causal reasoning, movement, specificity, and ease.

## 1. Finding hidden value: route density

The attractive part of this business is not simply that 65% of revenue comes back every year. It is what that recurrence could do to the economics if the routes are dense enough.

A technician who drives twelve minutes between stops can service meaningfully more customers in a day than one crossing half the county, even if both charge the same price and carry the same equipment. Add one customer on an existing street and much of that new revenue can fall through at a much higher incremental margin because the truck, technician, and drive time were already there.

That makes route density more than an operating detail. It is the mechanism that can turn modest local growth into operating leverage.

The open question is whether this company actually has it. Sixty-five percent recurring revenue spread across a sprawling service area is a very different asset from the same revenue packed into a handful of neighborhoods. Before underwriting a margin-expansion story, we would want the customer map, stops per technician per day, and drive time between jobs.

If those numbers are good, the existing customer book may be worth more than the headline multiple suggests.

### Why this works

It finds a positive mechanism rather than searching for a catch.

The paragraph begins with a stated fact, asks what makes that fact economically valuable, explains the operating mechanism in physical terms, identifies the missing evidence, and lands on price.

The reader learns something about route businesses without being told they are receiving a lesson.

## 2. Recurring revenue comes in grades

The listing says 70% of revenue is recurring, and in pest control that word is doing more work than it looks. A quarterly service agreement in this category is usually not a contract in any meaningful sense. It is often closer to a standing appointment the customer can cancel. Whether that is true here is something we would want to confirm.

If it is, the question is not whether the revenue recurs but why it recurs. Our working hypothesis would be inertia: the bugs stayed gone, the technician was polite, and nobody shops for a new exterminator while things are working.

That is a real asset, but a different one from a multiyear contract. If the relationship lives partly in the technician rather than the company, turnover can matter more than the word "recurring" suggests.

The general lesson is simple: recurring revenue comes in grades, and the grade depends on what holds it in place. A signed term, a switching cost, and a habit are not economically identical. Habit deserves value. It also deserves a different multiple.

### Why this works

It is skeptical without becoming negative.

It separates the observed listing claim from the mechanism we are inferring.

It turns a familiar finance term into something the reader can reason about.

## 3. Opening hook

The broker is selling a $3 million plumbing company. The interesting asset may be eight miles of suburb.

That sounds strange until you look at how a service business earns its margin. A plumber who spends the morning driving between far-apart calls is selling less labor than one whose jobs stack neatly inside the same cluster of neighborhoods. The trucks can be identical. The hourly rate can be identical. The difference is geography.

The listing gives us revenue, SDE, and a large repeat-customer base. It does not give us the map. For this deal, the map may matter almost as much as the P&L.

### Why this works

The opening makes a concrete claim that creates curiosity.

It does not begin with industry size, generic market growth, or a summary of the listing.

It introduces the analytical mechanism before introducing the furniture.

## 4. Explaining a genuinely good business

There is a temptation to search every small-business listing for the trick. Here, the more useful observation may be that the obvious thing is real.

Customers prepay for annual inspections. The work requires limited inventory. The equipment burden is light. A technician can complete multiple jobs in a day, and most of the cost base is labor that scales with the work. Nothing in that description is exotic. Put together, though, it means the company converts revenue to cash with unusually little friction.

That matters because growth does not appear to demand another dollar of inventory for every dollar of sales or a fleet-expansion plan before the next customer can be served. If the listing is directionally representative, the owner is not merely reporting a strong margin. The business architecture helps explain why the margin could be real.

We would still want to know what the owner personally does and how much of the customer base is repeat. But those are questions about transferability and durability. They do not erase the underlying point: this looks like a very good cash machine.

### Why this works

Positive depth means explaining why excellence exists.

The passage does not treat praise as a conclusion that needs less evidence than criticism.

## 5. Bear case without harshness

The clean version of the thesis assumes that the owner can be replaced without disturbing the revenue base. That is also the place we are most likely to be wrong.

The listing calls the owner "semi-absentee," but that phrase can cover almost anything from signing checks twice a week to being the person every large customer calls when a job goes sideways. If the second version is closer to reality, our replacement-cost adjustment is too small and the transferability score is too generous.

There is a second-order problem too. The strongest customers may not formally belong to the owner, but years of exception-handling can make the relationship personal anyway. A capable operations manager can replace tasks faster than trust.

None of this means the business is owner-dependent. It means the deal turns on where judgment and relationships actually live. The next disclosure should make that visible quickly.

### Why this works

The Bear Case is strong without pretending the feared outcome is already true.

It criticizes the underwriting assumption, not the seller.

It ends with the evidence needed to resolve the issue.

## 6. Price conclusion

At $2.8 million, the listing looks like a 3.5x SDE deal. On our Year 0 reconstruction, it is closer to 4.6x to 5.1x owner-independent cash earnings.

That difference is the deal.

We can imagine a version of this company earning materially more under New Management. Pricing looks unsophisticated, the route structure may support better density, and one adjacent service appears to fit the same customer base. But those are our ideas. The seller does not get paid today for a margin we still have to create.

On the business as it appears to exist now, we would be interested in the low-$2 millions, not the high-$2 millions. At that price the existing cash flow can carry the debt, the downside has room, and the upside belongs to the buyer who executes it.

The Bet survives. The asking price does not.

### Why this works

The paragraph makes valuation feel like the consequence of the analysis rather than a detached multiple exercise.

It cleanly separates Year 0 value from buyer-created upside.

## 7. Multi-paragraph sequencing and visual rhythm

The first thing to notice about this company is that the machines are not the business. Utilization is.

A shop can own a beautiful equipment base and still produce mediocre returns if the machines spend half the week waiting for jobs, setups, operators, or materials. That is why the broker's stated equipment value matters less to us than the hours those assets are actually earning money.

**Style:** New Management house illustration style. Warm hand-drawn gouache and colored-pencil character on cream paper, miniature-world feel, cheerful controlled palette.  
**Avoid:** Company logos, readable fake labels, or anything implying this is the actual seller's facility.  
**Aspect:** 3:2 landscape.

The listing reports $4.2 million of revenue on roughly $2.1 million of stated equipment. That ratio is only a clue. We do not know machine hours, setup time, scrap, or where the bottleneck sits. But it tells us exactly what we want next: whether the asset base is scarce productive capacity or simply a large amount of capital sitting on the floor.

The reconstruction makes the listing look less cheap, but it also sharpens the opportunity. If the shop has spare productive hours rather than obsolete capacity, the best growth dollar may be the one that fills a machine we already own.

That is a very different New Management plan from buying more equipment.

### Why this works

The sequence changes mode naturally:

setup → mechanism → visual break → evidence → exhibit → interpretation.

The prose does not repeat the table.

The image is not decorative. It makes the mechanism easier to feel.

The final line advances the investment view.
