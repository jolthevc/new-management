# Screen Card Examples

Status: Living reference  
Purpose: Show the cheap-screen stage what good judgment looks like without letting it perform full underwriting.

The Screen Card helps James decide whether a listing deserves expensive research.

It does not decide for him.

It does not browse the web.

It does not reconstruct a P&L.

It does not pretend to know facts the listing does not state.

## Good example: route-based service

### Full Screen Card

**Commercial HVAC service | Phoenix, AZ**  
**Ask:** $2.4M · **Revenue:** $4.8M · **SDE:** $720K · **Headline multiple:** 3.3x

A long-established commercial HVAC service business with a stated mix of recurring maintenance and repair work. The listing describes 14 employees and calls the owner semi-absentee, but does not specify what the owner still handles.

**Why interesting:** The combination of maintenance revenue, field-service labor, and a relatively strong stated SDE margin creates a potentially attractive route-and-technician economics story. If the recurring work is genuinely durable and geographically concentrated, incremental customers may be more valuable than the headline margin alone suggests.

**Immediate concerns:** "Semi-absentee" is doing a lot of work without a role description. The listing also does not say whether maintenance relationships are contractual, habitual, or concentrated.

**Likely angle:** Cash Cow / Platform. The interesting question is whether the maintenance book is a transferable company asset or still partly an owner-and-technician relationship asset.

**Why it deserves attention:** There is enough here to support a real pre-NDA thesis, and the unanswered questions are economically important rather than merely missing detail.

[Listing]

### Digest Card

**Commercial HVAC | Phoenix**  
$2.4M ask · $4.8M revenue · $720K SDE · 3.3x. Maintenance-heavy field-service model with a potentially interesting route-density and recurrence story. Watch the "semi-absentee" claim and whether recurring work is contractual or simply repeat behavior. Angle: Cash Cow / Platform.

## Good example: thin consumer listing

### Full Screen Card

**Neighborhood restaurant | Raleigh, NC**  
**Ask:** $850K · **Revenue:** $2.1M · **SDE:** $310K · **Headline multiple:** 2.7x

A single-location restaurant in operation for more than a decade. The listing gives basic financials and seating capacity but little detail on lease terms, concept strength, owner hours, or revenue mix.

**Why interesting:** The stated multiple is not obviously demanding, and a long operating history can be meaningful in a category where weak concepts disappear quickly. The listing is also thin enough that the real question is whether there is anything structurally special behind the earnings.

**Immediate concerns:** Restaurants can look cash-generative while carrying lease, labor, maintenance, and owner-dependence risks that are invisible in SDE. None of those can be resolved from this listing.

**Likely angle:** Cash Cow or operator-dependent business.

**Why it deserves attention:** Probably not a priority unless the location, lease economics, or concept creates a stronger reason to care. The cheap screen should not manufacture one.

[Listing]

### Why this works

The card is willing to say "probably not" without pretending the listing has a hidden flaw.

## Bad example: summary only

> This is a profitable HVAC company with $4.8 million of revenue and $720,000 of SDE. The company has recurring revenue and operates in a growing market. The asking price is 3.3x SDE, which appears reasonable. Risks include owner dependence and competition. Overall, this could be an attractive acquisition.

### Why it fails

It simply rephrases the listing.

It adds generic claims about the market.

It does not identify the economic question that would justify expensive work.

## Bad example: accidental deep underwriting

> We estimate normalized EBITDA at $510,000 after deducting $150,000 of owner replacement and $60,000 of maintenance capex. At a 4.0x multiple the business is worth $2.04 million.

### Why it fails

The cheap screen has neither the research nor evidence required to make those assumptions.

That work belongs after James selects the deal.

## Screen Card standard

A strong card should answer:

- What is it?
- What numbers did the listing actually state?
- What might make this economically interesting?
- What is the most important immediate uncertainty visible from the listing?
- What analytical angle might be worth pursuing?
- Why does this listing deserve attention, or why probably not?

It should leave James wanting either to click SELECT or move on.

Nothing more.
