# Under New Management Examples

Status: Living reference  
Purpose: Demonstrate the quality ladder for value creation.

Use governance/08_value_creation_standard.md for the rules.

These examples show what specificity and originality feel like.

Do not copy the ideas into unrelated businesses.

## Example 1: Route-based service company

### Generic

> Raise prices and improve route efficiency.

Why it fails:

The statement names two desirable outcomes without explaining what would actually change.

### Mechanical

> Reprice the oldest maintenance cohorts at renewal rather than raising every customer at once. Use the first cohort to measure churn and gross-margin lift before extending the program. In parallel, redraw technician territories around customer density rather than historical technician ownership of accounts, with the goal of increasing stops per paid field hour.

Why it passes:

Both ideas identify an operating mechanism.

Pricing moves realized revenue per stop.

Territory redesign moves productive stops per technician.

Both can be tested.

### Structural

> Treat density as the product. Stop accepting every profitable-looking job at the edge of the service area and concentrate customer acquisition inside the ZIP codes where the company already has route mass. Over time, the company becomes a tighter local network rather than a wider service map. The benefit is not only fewer windshield hours. Denser routes can support faster response, more jobs per truck, better technician economics, and a stronger local service promise.

What has to be true:

- enough demand exists inside the target clusters;
- current routes are meaningfully fragmented;
- customers do not require broad geographic coverage;
- technician territories can be changed without damaging relationships.

Why it may remain uncaptured:

The owner may have optimized for accepting revenue rather than maximizing density, or customer acquisition may historically have been referral-led rather than geographically managed. That is a hypothesis, not a known seller motive.

Why it is differentiated:

A typical buyer may see "grow revenue." This idea asks whether intentionally refusing some revenue could make the remaining network more valuable.

Bank test:

Passes if the customer map and route economics support it.

## Example 2: Small manufacturer

### Generic

> Use automation to improve margins.

Why it fails:

"Automation" is a technology label, not an investment idea.

### Mechanical

> Measure setup hours and scrap on the highest-volume part families. If a small number of repeat jobs drive disproportionate setup time, invest first in fixtures and standardized setups rather than new machine capacity. The target is more sellable machine hours from the installed base.

What has to be true:

- setup time is a real bottleneck;
- repeat part families are common enough to standardize;
- the bottleneck is not actually operator availability or a downstream process.

Why it may remain uncaptured:

Small shops often add machines when schedules feel full because machine capacity is visible and setup loss is not. That is a category hypothesis and should be tested here.

### Structural

> Shift the commercial model toward work that values scarce process capability rather than raw machine hours. If the shop owns one unusually difficult certification, process, or quality capability, use that scarcity to move mix toward jobs where the customer is buying confidence and qualification rather than commodity capacity.

What has to be true:

- the capability is genuinely scarce;
- customers value it;
- the shop can sell into those accounts;
- qualification does not require a multi-year investment cycle.

Why it is differentiated:

It changes what the company sells, not simply how efficiently it produces the current mix.

## Example 3: Commercial service business

### Generic

> Add adjacent services.

Why it fails:

Almost every local-service memo can say this.

### Mechanical

> Identify customers buying the core service at multiple sites but purchasing the adjacent service elsewhere. Start with the top twenty existing accounts, sell the adjacency through the current account relationship, and outsource fulfillment initially so the company can test attachment before adding fixed cost.

Why it passes:

It names the customer, channel, sequencing, and risk-control mechanism.

### Structural

> Reframe the company from a single-service vendor into the customer's outsourced maintenance layer, but only around adjacencies that share the same buyer, visit cadence, and field footprint. The value is not "more services." It is increasing revenue per customer without rebuilding acquisition cost or route infrastructure.

What has to be true:

- the same buyer controls the adjacent spend;
- service quality can be maintained;
- the adjacency does not create a fundamentally different labor model;
- the company has enough customer trust to cross-sell.

## Example 4: What to leave alone

Under New Management should not mean changing everything.

Good:

> Day 1, we would leave the technician compensation model alone. The listing's strongest clue is low stated turnover, and without employee-level evidence we have no reason to disturb the system that may be producing it. The first ninety days should be spent understanding why technicians stay before importing a "better" incentive plan.

Why this matters:

Restraint is an operating decision.

A generic analysis often confuses activity with value creation.

## Example 5: A differentiated idea that should be rejected

Idea:

> Turn service visits into a theatrical branded experience with costumes, social content, and a viral customer-referral program.

Why it fails:

It may be creative, but there is no clear connection to the Business Physics, no evidence customers want it, and no serious operator would underwrite the acquisition around it.

This fails the bank test.

Novelty is not value creation.

## Final standard

A strong Under New Management section should contain fewer, better levers.

Each surviving idea should be specific enough to act on, grounded enough to underwrite, and tied tightly enough to the business that it could not simply be pasted into the next listing.
